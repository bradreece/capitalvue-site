In a seller's market, losing means being outbid. In this market, it almost never does. If you missed the last one, the cause is probably on your side of the contract, and that is better news than it sounds, because it is the side you control.

Here is the shape of the market you lost in. Capital city homes are taking a median of 39 days to sell, against 23 a year ago. Advertised stock is up 23.1% on a year earlier. The combined capital city auction clearance rate was 45.4% in the week to 4 October, 22 percentage points below the same week last year. Brisbane cleared 21.2%, its lowest since November 2008. Adelaide cleared 32.9%, against 78.5% a year earlier.

Those are not the numbers of a market where buyers get beaten to the line. They are the numbers of a market where most properties do not sell on the day, most offers are the only offer, and the buyer who misses out has usually done it to themselves.

It is rarely the money

When we sit down with someone who has lost a property, the story they tell is about price. Someone paid more. The agent wanted more. They could not stretch.

When we go back through the file, price is the cause in a minority of cases. The rest fall into five patterns, and they repeat.

  1. Too early. The offer went in before the evidence did.
  2. Too low. The offer was anchored to the guide, which is not a price.
  3. Wrong conditions. The offer carried terms the vendor could not accept, or could accept from someone else more easily.
  4. Wrong day. The property went to auction and a decision got made in ninety seconds that should have taken a week.
  5. Walked away. The right one was there, and the buyer talked themselves out of it.

Every one of those is a decision, not a bank balance. Which means every one of them changes next time.

Too early: offering before the evidence is in

The fastest way to lose a negotiation is to open it without knowing what the thing is worth. An offer made on the strength of the listing photos, the guide and a single walk-through is an offer made on the vendor's information, and the vendor's agent knows it.

Evidence means settled comparable sales, same suburb, same type, recent, and the overlays, the body corporate records and the building report read before the number goes in, not after. Our due diligence checklist sets out what that looks like in Queensland and South Australia. A buyer who has done it can hold a number under pressure. A buyer who has not will move on the first counter, and the agent will have seen that coming.

Too low: anchoring to the guide

The price guide is a marketing decision. It is set to generate enquiry, and in a falling market it is often set above where the vendor would actually sell, because the vendor's expectations were formed in a market that ended in March. Why there is no price on the listing covers what each form of the guide is for.

The buyer who anchors to it loses in two directions. Offer at the guide and you may be paying above the evidence. Offer a token amount under it and you have told the agent you are not serious, and the agent stops returning calls. Neither is a negotiation. The number that holds is the one the comparable sales support, stated once, with the reasoning, and left there.

Wrong conditions: why the clean offer wins

Two offers, same price. One is subject to finance with a 21-day clause, subject to a building and pest inspection, and asks for a 90-day settlement. The other has finance already assessed, the inspection done, and offers a 30-day settlement. The vendor's agent does not need to think about it.

Conditions are where a buyer loses to a lower offer, and it happens more in this market, not less. A vendor who has been on the market for 39 days and has one offer in hand will still take a cleaner one at a lower price, because the risk of the first one falling over and starting again is worth more to them than the difference.

The finance clause is the one that decides most of these, and it is the one most buyers could have removed before the search began. An assessed pre-approval lets you shorten the finance condition or drop it. We set out what that means in pre-approval, explained without a loan to sell. A buyer without one cannot offer clean terms, whatever the price, and in a market where vendors are choosing on certainty rather than on price, that is the difference.

Settlement length matters more than buyers think. In a falling market a vendor wants the money sooner, not later. Offering a short settlement costs you nothing if your finance is in place and it is often worth more to the vendor than a few thousand dollars.

Wrong day: the auction

A year ago the risk at auction was being outbid. With Brisbane clearing one in five and Adelaide one in three, the risk has moved. Most auctions now end with the property passed in, and the real negotiation happens in the ten minutes afterwards, between the vendor's agent and whoever is standing closest.

That is where buyers lose now. Either they bid against nobody, because the auctioneer's opening bid was a vendor bid and they did not know it, or they let the property pass in and then negotiate the post-auction price on the spot, without evidence, without a walk-away number, in front of an agent whose job is to close that afternoon. There is no cooling-off period on an auction purchase in either Queensland or South Australia. The decision is final the moment the hammer falls, or the moment you sign in the agent's car afterwards.

The buyer who walks in with a ceiling set from the sales evidence, knows what a vendor bid looks like, and has decided before the day whether they will negotiate after a pass-in or walk, does not lose at auction. They either buy inside their number or leave. Both are results.

Walked away: the one you regret

Sometimes the loss is the one you did not make an offer on. The property ticked every box, the evidence supported the price, and you talked yourself out of it. Too soon. Something better will come. The market is falling, wait.

Regret is information. If you are still thinking about a property three weeks later, your criteria were right and your nerve was wrong, and the fix is not a different property, it is a decision made before the next one appears about what you will do when the evidence lines up. Waiting is a strategy only if you know what you are waiting for. Otherwise it is a habit, and in a market with more stock than buyers it is an expensive one, because the thing that makes this market negotiable is the same thing that ends it.

What changes next time

The pattern across all five is the same. The decision that lost the property was made during the search, under time pressure, with the agent in the room. The fix is to make it before the search, when there is no pressure and no agent.

Before the next one: finance assessed, so the conditions can be clean. The ceiling set from the evidence, so the number can be held. The auction plan decided, so the day does not decide it for you. And a clear answer to the question of what you will do when the right one appears, so that walking away is a choice rather than a reflex.
